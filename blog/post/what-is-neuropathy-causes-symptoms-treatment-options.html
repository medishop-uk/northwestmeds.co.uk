What is neuropathy? Causes, symptoms & treatment options

Millions of people live with neuropathy every day, yet many don't fully understand what's happening inside their bodies or what they can do about it. Neuropathy, at its core, is nerve damage that disrupts the signals your nervous system relies on to function.

Whether you're recently diagnosed or researching on behalf of a loved one, this guide from NorthwestMeds breaks down everything you need to know: what neuropathy is, why it happens, how it feels, and the treatments available today.

What is neuropathy?

Neuropathy — often called peripheral neuropathy — is a condition that results from damage to the peripheral nervous system, the vast network of nerves outside your brain and spinal cord. These nerves act like electrical cables, sending messages between your central nervous system and the rest of your body. When those cables are damaged, the signals get scrambled, slowed, or cut off entirely.

  • Peripheral neuropathy affects the hands, feet, legs, and arms — the most common form.
  • Autonomic neuropathy damages nerves that control automatic body functions like heart rate, digestion, and bladder control.
  • Focal neuropathy targets a single nerve or group of nerves, causing sudden, localized weakness or pain.

What Causes Neuropathy?

Neuropathy doesn't have a single cause — it can develop from a wide range of medical conditions, lifestyle factors, and even unknown origins. Understanding the root cause is the first step toward effective management.

  • Diabetes is the leading cause of neuropathy worldwide. High blood sugar gradually damages nerve fibers, particularly in the feet and legs—a condition known as diabetic peripheral neuropathy.
  • Autoimmune diseases such as lupus, rheumatoid arthritis, and Guillain-Barré syndrome can cause the immune system to attack nerve tissue directly.
  • Vitamin deficiencies — especially B1, B6, B12, and E — are critical to nerve health. A prolonged deficiency can lead to significant nerve deterioration.
  • Infections including shingles (herpes zoster), Lyme disease, HIV, and hepatitis C are known to inflame and damage peripheral nerves.
  • Toxic exposure to heavy metals like lead or mercury, as well as long-term alcohol abuse, introduces substances that are directly harmful to nerve fibers.
  • Medications — particularly certain chemotherapy drugs, antiretrovirals, and some antibiotics — list neuropathy as a known side effect.
  • Hereditary conditions like Charcot-Marie-Tooth disease are inherited forms of neuropathy passed down through families.
  • Idiopathic neuropathy refers to cases where no identifiable cause can be found, accounting for up to 30% of all diagnoses.

Recognising the Symptoms of Neuropathy

Neuropathy symptoms vary depending on which nerves are affected, but there are hallmark signs that most people experience. Recognising them early can make a real difference in slowing progression and improving quality of life.

  • Tingling or "pins and needles" — a persistent prickling sensation, most often in the hands and feet, that doesn't go away the way normal numbness does after sitting awkwardly.
  • Numbness — a gradual loss of sensation that can make it difficult to feel temperature, texture, or pain, increasing the risk of unnoticed injuries.
  • Burning or shooting pain — a sharp, electric-like pain or deep burning that can be constant or come in waves, often worsening at night.
  • Muscle weakness — damaged motor nerves lead to muscle weakness, difficulty with coordination, or trouble performing everyday tasks like gripping objects or climbing stairs.
  • Sensitivity to touch — even the lightest contact, such as a bed sheet resting on the skin, can cause disproportionate pain (a condition called allodynia).
  • Balance and coordination problems — loss of proprioception (the body's sense of position) makes walking unsteady and increases fall risk significantly.
  • Autonomic symptoms — when autonomic nerves are involved, symptoms can include excessive sweating, digestive issues, blood pressure fluctuations, or bladder dysfunction.

How Is Neuropathy Diagnosed?

Diagnosing neuropathy requires a combination of clinical assessment and targeted testing. No single test confirms it outright — doctors piece together a picture from multiple sources.

  • Medical history and physical exam form the first line of assessment, looking at symptom patterns, risk factors, and neurological reflexes.
  • Blood tests help identify underlying causes such as diabetes, vitamin deficiencies, thyroid disorders, or autoimmune markers.
  • Nerve conduction studies (NCS) measure the speed and strength of electrical signals moving through your nerves, pinpointing areas of dysfunction.
  • Electromyography (EMG) records the electrical activity in muscles to distinguish nerve damage from muscle disease.
  • Skin biopsy or nerve biopsy may be used in complex cases to examine nerve fiber density or structural abnormalities at a cellular level.

Treatment options for Neuropathy

While there is currently no universal cure for neuropathy, a range of treatments can effectively manage symptoms, slow nerve damage, and significantly improve day-to-day function. At NorthwestMeds, we believe in comprehensive, patient-centred care — and that starts with understanding your options.

  • Treating the underlying cause is always the priority. Managing blood sugar in diabetics, supplementing deficient vitamins, or discontinuing a causative medication can halt or even partially reverse neuropathy.
  • Prescription medications such as gabapentin, pregabalin (Lyrica), duloxetine (Cymbalta), and tricyclic antidepressants are commonly used to reduce nerve pain and improve sleep quality.
  • Topical treatments including lidocaine patches and capsaicin cream offer localised relief with fewer systemic side effects, making them a popular first-line option for mild cases.
  • Physical therapy helps rebuild strength, improve balance, and retrain the nervous system — reducing the risk of falls and maintaining independence.
  • Pain management techniques such as transcutaneous electrical nerve stimulation (TENS), acupuncture, and nerve blocks can provide relief when medications alone are insufficient.
  • Lifestyle modifications — including reducing alcohol intake, following an anti-inflammatory diet, quitting smoking, and exercising regularly — support nerve health and slow disease progression.
  • Supplements like alpha-lipoic acid, B-complex vitamins, and acetyl-L-carnitine have shown promise in clinical studies for reducing neuropathy symptoms, especially in diabetic patients.

Living with neuropathy: practical tips

Managing neuropathy is an ongoing process, but with the right approach, many people lead full and active lives. Small changes can have a big impact on daily comfort and safety.

  • Inspect your feet daily if you have reduced sensation — cuts and sores can go unnoticed and become serious infections without proper monitoring.
  • Wear well-fitted, protective footwear to prevent pressure sores and injuries to numb areas.
  • Use assistive devices such as grab bars, non-slip mats, and orthotic insoles to reduce fall risk at home.
  • Keep a symptom journal to track patterns, triggers, and the effectiveness of treatments—this information is invaluable for your healthcare team.
  • Stay consistent with appointments — neuropathy requires regular monitoring to adjust treatment plans as the condition evolves.

FAQ about neuropathy

What are the most common symptoms of neuropathy?

Common symptoms include tingling, burning pain, numbness, muscle weakness, and increased sensitivity in the hands or feet.

What causes neuropathy or nerve damage?

Neuropathy can be caused by diabetes, injuries, infections, vitamin deficiencies, or certain medications and health conditions.

Can neuropathy go away on its own?

Some mild cases may improve, but many people need treatment to manage symptoms and prevent further nerve damage.

What medications are commonly used to treat neuropathy pain?

Doctors often prescribe medications such as pregabalin, gabapentin, or other nerve pain relief treatments depending on the condition.

When should I see a doctor about neuropathy symptoms?

You should seek medical advice if you have ongoing numbness, burning pain, weakness, or symptoms that interfere with daily life.
